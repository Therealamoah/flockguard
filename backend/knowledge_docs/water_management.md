# Poultry Drinking Water Primer

Authors: Brian D. Fairchild and Casey W. Ritz, Extension Poultry Scientists, University of Georgia Extension (Bulletin 1301, revised April 2015).

Water is a critical nutrient that receives little attention until a problem arises. Producers should provide water in adequate quantity and know what is in the water flowing through the water lines used in evaporative cooling systems and consumed by the birds.

## Water Functions

Water is needed for bird consumption, reducing air temperature (including evaporative cooling pad and fogging systems), and facility sanitation. Broilers consume approximately 1.6 to 2.0 times as much water as feed on a weight basis. Water consumed by the bird is used for nutrient transportation, enzymatic and chemical reactions in the body, body temperature regulation, and lubrication of joints and organs.

There is a strong relationship between feed and water consumption, so water can be used to monitor flock performance. A water meter installed separately on lines supplying the front and rear of the house can help monitor bird uniformity - water consumption will be greater in the area with more birds. When birds are not distributed evenly, competition for feed and water space increases, which, combined with extra heat from excessive bird numbers, can reduce performance.

## Factors Affecting Water Consumption

- **Bird age**: Water consumption increases with age but decreases as a percentage of body weight.
- **Environmental temperature / heat stress**: Birds consume more water as temperature increases (they regulate body temperature partly by evaporating water through the respiratory system while panting). Water consumption can double or triple during heat stress, and increases approximately 7% for each degree Fahrenheit increase in temperature. A University of Georgia study of seven consecutive commercial broiler flocks found water consumed per pound of feed consumed rose with temperature: 1.55 lb water/lb feed in cold weather, 1.65 in mild weather, 1.75 in hot weather.
- **Water temperature**: Water below the bird's body temperature is beneficial - it helps dissipate body heat - though cooling water significantly over long house runs is difficult in practice.
- **Electrolytes**: Producers often supplement drinking water with electrolytes during heat stress to help regulate nerve/muscle function and acid-base balance, and to stimulate water consumption; this can help reduce heat-stress mortality.
- **Lighting programs**: Birds generally don't drink if they aren't eating, and vice versa - they rest and don't consume water much during dark periods (except long dark periods over 8 hours). Under lighting programs, two water-consumption peaks are typically seen: just after lights come on (dawn) and just before lights turn off (dusk).

Failure to provide adequate, unlimited water access results in reduced feed intake, poor egg production, reduced growth, and reduced feed efficiency.

## Water Quality

Water is a universal solvent and can contain many minerals and compounds; poultry drinking water doesn't need to be pure, but heavily contaminated water is undesirable. Composition varies by region and can be affected by surface-water contamination draining into a well. All farms should have well water tested by a qualified laboratory to establish a water-quality baseline.

Poor water quality can interfere with digestion and bird performance, reduce the effectiveness of vaccines/medications delivered through water lines, cause equipment problems (leaky nipples, clogged foggers), and lead to wet litter and increased ammonia production.

Factors to monitor for poultry water quality:

- **Color**: water is normally colorless; any color may indicate contamination.
- **Turbidity**: suspended particles (clay, silt, sand, organic material) cause cloudy/muddy water; can cause leaky nipples and clogged fogging nozzles.
- **Hardness**: calcium/magnesium salts cause scale/sludge buildup in lines and reduce effectiveness of soaps, disinfectants, and some medications.
- **Iron**: stains and is a common issue; doesn't appear to directly affect bird health at studied levels, but solid particulates (iron oxide) can block nipples/foggers and iron bacteria can cause biofilm buildup.
- **Manganese**: similar to iron - not directly harmful to birds at studied levels, but can form solid particulates causing equipment problems.
- **Nitrate**: elevated levels indicate decaying organic material; studies found no broiler performance differences up to 600 ppm, but nitrate presence is a good indicator to check for bacteria.
- **pH**: preferred range 6.0-6.8 for broiler production (birds tolerate 4-8); pH above 8 can reduce water consumption.
- **Alkalinity**: caused by calcium carbonate/bicarbonate/sulfate; high alkalinity increases water's buffering capacity.
- **Total solids**: not directly linked to bird health issues, but can affect equipment function and water delivery.
- **Toxic compounds** (lead, selenium, arsenic, etc.): should be kept below 1.0 ppm.
- **Dissolved oxygen**: normal groundwater has little to none; concentrations above zero may indicate surface-water influence.
- **Bacteria**: should be kept to a minimum (see guideline table below).

### Suggested maximum acceptable levels (guide only, not a regulatory standard)

| Contaminant | Maximum acceptable level |
|---|---|
| Total Heterotrophic Bacteria | 100 CFU/100 ml |
| Coliform Bacteria | 50 CFU/100 ml |
| pH | 5.0 - 6.8 |
| Hardness | 110 ppm |
| Calcium | 500 ppm |
| Chloride | 250 ppm |
| Copper | 0.6 ppm |
| Iron | 0.3 ppm |
| Magnesium | 125 ppm |
| Manganese | 0.05 ppm |
| Nitrate | 25 ppm |
| Phosphorus | 0.1 ppm |
| Potassium | 500 ppm |
| Sodium | 50 ppm |
| Sulfate | 250 ppm |

Note: research cited in the source found iron as high as 600 ppm, manganese as high as 20 ppm, and nitrates as high as 600 ppm did not directly affect bird health in studies, but higher levels than the guide table above can still cause equipment problems (leaky nipples, clogged foggers) even without a direct health effect.

## Water Management Tips

- **Conduct water tests**: test well water regularly, and especially during heavy rain or drought, when quality can change. A county extension agent can advise on available tests and submission to a qualified lab.
- **Change filters regularly**: sediment/particulates cause leaky nipples and clogged filters restrict flow to drinkers and cooling systems; some water (e.g. high iron) may need treatment beyond simple cartridge filters.
- **Flush water lines regularly**: a high-pressure flush should be performed between each flock and after adding supplements through the medicator (vaccines, medications, vitamins, electrolytes).
- **Plan ahead before treating water**: consult a county agent before implementing water treatment/sanitation programs to avoid contaminants reacting negatively and clogging the system.
