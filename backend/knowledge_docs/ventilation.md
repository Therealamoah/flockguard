# Managing Heat and Minimum Ventilation Systems in the Broiler House

Authors: Tom Tabler (Mississippi State University Poultry Science), Yi Liang (University of Arkansas), Jonathan R. Moyle (University of Maryland Extension), F. Dustan Clark (University of Arkansas Cooperative Extension), Morgan Farnell (MSU Poultry Science), Jessica Wells (MSU Poultry Science). Mississippi State University Extension, Publication 2854.

Ventilation is necessary to provide the healthy environmental conditions broilers need to thrive, but adequate ventilation often means balancing fuel use against keeping birds warm enough. Growing birds too cool forces them to consume excess feed for body-temperature maintenance instead of growth, harming feed conversion and flock performance - and mistakes made in the first few days of a flock's life (when growth potential is highest) cannot be compensated for later.

## Heating System

The heating system is one of the most important items in the house - even in summer, some supplemental heat is needed at night for baby chicks, and it is critical for chick survival in winter. A heater that doesn't work properly wastes fuel and costs both fuel expense and lost bird performance, regardless of heater type (jet brooder, tube heater, space heater, infrared brooder).

**Common maintenance issues to check for:**
- Low gas pressure from poorly functioning regulators (recognizable by a weak yellow flame instead of a strong blue one). Propane systems typically need 10-12 inches of water column pressure; natural gas systems typically need 6-8 inches.
- Clogged burner and pilot orifices (check for mud dauber nests, spider webs, or other obstructions).
- Misaligned direct spark igniters causing brooder ignition failure.
- Leaking hoses and fittings (check with soapy water/solution - bubbles indicate a leak; never use an open flame to check for gas leaks).
- Undersized gas piping, which can starve brooders near the end of the line, causing sputtering/unstable burning and reduced heat output.
- Soot buildup on brooders, which indicates inefficient burning, wastes fuel, and produces excess carbon monoxide.

**Heat source types:**
- Forced air furnaces heat by convection - now the least preferred brooding method.
- Pancake/jet-type brooders transfer some heat by convection and some as radiant heat to surfaces.
- Radiant brooders transfer most heat to surfaces rather than warming the air; they operate higher off the floor (4-5 ft vs. 3 ft for pancake brooders) and create wider "comfort zones" for chicks to choose from.
- Radiant tube heat: hot air from a burner is forced down a metal pipe, which radiates heat; tubes usually hang high near the ceiling with reflectors directing heat downward, giving more uniform floor temperature with less of a "hot spot."

**Between-flock maintenance:** clean the heating system after every flock (dust reduces efficiency - an air compressor or leaf blower helps); check gas hoses for burn damage, dry rot, or kinking; keep load-out and entrance doors closed to prevent wild birds (sparrows, starlings) nesting in brooders, and always check for nests before lighting brooders if wild birds may have had access. Sensors/thermostats should be placed away from excessive heat, drafts, or fan airflow to avoid inaccurate readings.

## Minimum Ventilation System

Alongside heating, the minimum ventilation system (fans, sidewall air inlets, vent machines) must be mastered. The number/size of air inlets must be matched to fan size/number to maintain correct static pressure, pulling cold outside air through high inlets and keeping it along the ceiling until it reaches the center of the house for proper mixing and warming.

**Rule of thumb:** for every 0.01 inch of static pressure, air travels about 2 feet. To move air to the center of a 40-foot-wide house while keeping it near the ceiling requires roughly 0.10 inch of pressure - meaning the house should be able to pull 0.15 inch or more of pressure at 1 cfm per square foot of floor space during a fan-powered tightness test. Incorrect vent-door width causes cold air to hit chicks directly instead of tempering/mixing first. Hanging survey tape or streamers from the ceiling is a simple way to visualize airflow.

**Maintenance:** vent machines, air inlets, and cables/steel wire need near-constant adjustment due to continuous on/off cycling; gears and bearings should be greased regularly and hinges oiled to prevent rust/corrosion; keep covers and guards in place; periodically check that rodents/litter beetles aren't attacking insulation on vent doors, and that doors close fully (partial closure reduces house tightness and makes static pressure harder to maintain).

Keep spare parts on hand for self-repair (igniters, fan belts, feed line motors) since a malfunction overnight is performance that can never be regained, and repair help isn't always available on short notice.

## Summary

Modern broiler management requires thinking in hours, not days or weeks - mistakes during suboptimal conditions cannot be compensated for later, especially with heating and minimum ventilation. Cold birds use feed to stay warm instead of to grow. Air quality and litter quality both depend on the minimum ventilation system working correctly, and bird health and paw quality depend on air/litter quality in turn.
