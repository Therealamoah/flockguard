# Ross Broiler Management Handbook — Practical Stockmanship / Flock Inspection (excerpt)

Publisher: Aviagen (Ross Broiler Management Handbook, 2025 edition).

Note: the full handbook (144 pages) covers house design, brooding, nutrition, lighting, genetics, and processing. This excerpt keeps only "Practical Stockmanship" - Aviagen's own framework for how a person should physically inspect a flock - since it's the section most directly applicable to a farmer performing a Morning/Evening/Emergency Flock Check or a triggered inspection.

## Why direct observation matters alongside records

Body-weight and feed-conversion targets are usually similar across flocks of the same age, but each individual flock has different day-to-day management needs. If only farm records (growth, feed consumption, etc.) are monitored, important signals from the birds and their environment will be missed - **often, the first signs of a problem are subtle changes in bird behavior**, not yet visible in the numbers. Knowing what's normal for a specific flock is what makes it possible to notice a deviation quickly.

Flock environment and behavior should be observed at multiple points in the day, ideally by the same person, both during routine tasks and via inspections done specifically to observe behavior (not just to perform a chore).

## Before entering the house

Note the time and ambient climatic conditions - this helps judge whether fans, heaters, cool cells, and inlets should currently be running a certain way relative to their set points. When opening the door, notice whether it opens with slight resistance, no resistance, or high resistance - this reflects internal air pressure and therefore ventilation settings.

## On entry: LOOK, LISTEN, FEEL, SMELL

Enter slowly and pause until birds settle, then use all senses to assess flock condition:

**LOOK at:**
- **Bird distribution** — are specific areas being avoided (suggesting draft, cold, hot, or uneven lighting)?
- **Bird respiration** — are birds panting, and is it localized (suggesting an airflow/temperature issue in that area)?
- **Bird behavior** — normally, broilers split roughly evenly between drinking, feeding, and resting.
- **Bird health** — do birds look healthy on visual inspection? Signs of injury or feather damage?
- **Fans, cooling pads, litter condition, feeders/drinkers, lighting** — correct operation, wet/dry patches, capping/wet litter (leaking drinkers or excess cooling water), droppings texture (wet/loose vs. dry/solid, feed particles present), correct feeder/drinker height, leaks, feed spillage/quality, dark spots or incorrect light timing.

**LISTEN to:**
- **The birds** — snicking/sneezing, and whether vocalizations are appropriate for age and consistent with previous visits (vs. a vaccination response or a dusty/poor environment). Best assessed in the evening when background noise is lower.
- **Feeders** — augers/chains running smoothly and constantly; feed bridging in the bin.
- **Fans** — noisy bearings or loose belts (routine maintenance prevents air-quality issues).

**FEEL:**
- **The air** — stuffy/humid, cold, hot, fast-moving, or still - can indicate specific issues like insufficient minimum ventilation.
- **Feed** — excessive dustiness, pellets breaking down too easily.
- **Litter** — compress a handful; if it stays compacted (doesn't spring apart), that indicates excessive moisture, possibly a ventilation issue; dry litter stays friable and falls apart.

**SMELL:**
- **Feed** — fresh vs. musty.
- **Environment** — presence of ammonia (NH3) smell.

## Walking the house and handling individual birds

Walk the entire house (not just near the entrance) to check for variation in environment/behavior across the space. Get down to bird level; pick up any bird that doesn't move away on approach and assess whether it's sick or injured, and how many birds are affected. Watch how the flock moves around you - do birds return to fill the space after you pass through?

When periodically stopping to handle individual birds, assess:
- **Eyes** — clear, no irritation
- **Skin** — unblemished, no scratches
- **Breast** — unblemished, no blisters
- **Feathering** — clean, no feathers sticking out abnormally
- **Leg health** — hocks clean, no irritation marks
- **Feet** — footpads clean, no irritation markings
- **Vent** — clean, no signs of loose droppings
- **Beak and tongue** — no nasal discharge or feed stuck to beak, no tongue discoloration or mouth lesions
- **Crop** — are they feeding? Does it contain litter? Very hard or soft? (crop condition is an indicator of water availability)
- **General demeanor and alertness**

Compare what's observed against actual farm records (e.g. is the flock on target weight for age?) - irregularities should be investigated and an action plan developed.

## Practical takeaway for FlockGuard-style inspections

This Look/Listen/Feel/Smell framework, followed by a house walk-through and individual-bird handling checks, is a reasonable structure for an inspection checklist: environment and distribution first (look), then sound cues, then tactile checks (air, feed, litter), then smell, then a walk-through assessing individual bird condition against the list above. It reinforces that behavior/environment changes are often an *earlier* signal than a change in aggregate records like mortality or feed totals - consistent with prioritizing an inspection when behavior/activity is flagged even before other numbers have moved much.
